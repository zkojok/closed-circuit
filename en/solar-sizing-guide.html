How many panels and batteries does your home need?

The sizing method we use on real projects, in four steps with a full worked example.

By Zakaria Kojok · Communications & energy engineer · 7 min read

The most common mistake I see: someone buys "a 5 kW system" because the neighbour has one, and two months later the battery dies at 2 a.m. The right size does not come from the number of panels. It comes from your own consumption. Here is the method we use, step by step, with a worked example.

1. Work out your daily consumption (kWh)

For each appliance: power (W) × hours per day ÷ 1000. Fridges and freezers do not run all the time; the compressor runs about 40% of the time.

AppliancePowerHours/daykWh/day
Fridge150 W24 × 40%1.44
LED lighting200 W61.20
TV + router135 W5 / 240.96
Washing machine500 W10.50
Water pump750 W10.75
Fans, laptop, chargers——0.65
Total≈ 5.5 kWh

An electric water heater (2000 W) or an air conditioner changes everything. Two hours of water heater alone is 4 kWh, almost half the house. Run it on solar during the day only, or use a solar water heater.

2. Battery: how long must you last without sun?

Add up what you use at night and during grid cuts. In the example, 6 p.m. to 7 a.m. is about 3 kWh. You can use about 90% of a lithium (LiFePO4) battery, so you need at least 3.3 kWh usable.

One 5.12 kWh battery is enough, but two give you margin for cloudy days and a longer battery life, because the battery does not drain to the bottom every night.

3. Inverter: the biggest load at the same moment

The inverter is not sized from daily energy but from the largest load running at once: pump + fridge + washing machine + lights ≈ 1.7 kW. Pumps and fridges draw 3 to 5 times their power for a few seconds when they start, so in the example we choose a 5 kW hybrid inverter. It handles the start-up surge and leaves room for an air conditioner later.

4. Panels: size for winter, not summer

Lebanon gets about 5 peak sun hours in summer and about 3 in winter, with roughly 20% losses from heat, dust and cables.

PV power (kWp) = daily consumption ÷ (sun hours × 0.8)

Summer: 5.5 ÷ (5 × 0.8) = 1.4 kWp. Winter: 5.5 ÷ (3 × 0.8) = 2.3 kWp. So 4 × 580 W panels (2.3 kWp) cover the house and charge the battery even in winter.

Result for the example

  • Daily consumption: 5.5 kWh
  • Battery: 2 × 5.12 kWh lithium
  • Inverter: 5 kW hybrid
  • Panels: 4 × 580 W

5 expensive mistakes

  1. Big array, small battery: power all day, blackout at night.
  2. Sizing for summer only: the battery never fills in winter.
  3. Cheap lead-acid batteries: you can only use about 50%, and they die in two years.
  4. Thin DC cables: they heat up, waste energy and are a fire risk.
  5. No protection: you need a DC fuse, a breaker, an SPD and earthing.
