How to point a satellite dish: elevation, azimuth, skew

The three angles you need to lock onto any satellite, and the on-site steps an engineer follows.

By Zakaria Kojok · Broadcast & satellite engineer · 6 min read

To lock onto a satellite you set three angles: azimuth (compass direction), elevation (how high the dish looks) and LNB skew (rotation of the LNB). They depend on where you are and which satellite you want, so no single number works for everyone.

1. Azimuth: where the dish points

The angle from true north, clockwise. Geostationary satellites sit above the equator, so from the northern hemisphere they are all to the south: from south-west (e.g. Nilesat from Lebanon) to south-east (Asian satellites).

A compass points to magnetic north, not true north. In Lebanon the difference is about 5° east, so subtract 5° from the true azimuth to get your compass reading. Keep the compass away from steel and from the dish while reading.

2. Elevation: how high it looks

The dish angle above the horizon. The closer the satellite is to your own longitude, the higher it is. Check for buildings or trees in that direction.

Most home dishes are offset dishes: the signal reaches the LNB at an angle, so the dish looks almost upright. The setting on the scale = elevation − offset angle (usually 20° to 27°; check the dish datasheet).

3. LNB skew: polarization rotation

H and V signals arrive slightly tilted because you are not on the satellite's longitude. If the satellite is west of you, rotate the LNB clockwise; if it is east, counter-clockwise, seen from behind the dish looking toward the satellite. Circular polarization (L/R) needs no rotation.

Pointing steps on site

  1. Set the mast perfectly vertical with a spirit level. Any tilt here ruins everything later.
  2. Set elevation on the scale (after subtracting the offset).
  3. Point roughly to the azimuth and rotate the LNB to the skew.
  4. Sweep left and right very slowly (one degree at a time) until the meter shows the signal.
  5. Tighten the bolts, then fine-tune elevation and skew for best quality (MER or C/N), not best signal strength.
